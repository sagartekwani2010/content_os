## 1. Does it stick?

**Go with changes.** The premise is stronger than "My job on trial" as a series, weaker as a single reel, and the opener as written kills it.

| Dimension | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hook potential | 4 | "Layoffs are around every corner" is the saturated line your panel already rejected. |
| Emotional pull | 7 | "Sitting duck" to "doing something" is agency, which is rarer than fear. |
| Credibility | 8 | He audits his own job first and has a record of showing failures. |
| Brand fit | 9 | Measure, offload, re-measure is the Gym Jarvis loop applied to a career. |
| Series potential | 7 | A monthly re-run is a real appointment, but only if the delta is visible and dated. |
| Participation | 5 | "Assess your job" is homework; it needs a 60-second, three-task version. |
| Sends | 6 | People send a specific task row ("this is you"), not a framework. |

**Versus "My job on trial" v6**
- **Better:** it has an engine (monthly loop, offload, reinvest), it is first-person and done before shown, and it ends in action rather than a verdict.
- **Worse:** the open is abstract, the tool demo with "factors" is a retention sink, and non-IT viewers hear nothing about themselves by 6 s. Keep the v6 race cold open and task rows.

## 2. Strongest version

**Emotional core:** agency through measurement. "I don't know if AI takes my job, but I know which 4 of my 11 tasks it can do today, and I check every month." Fear is only the context.

**Reel 1 owns one idea:** "I put my own job through an AI audit. Here is the dated score, and here is the one task I handed over this week."

It needs five things:
- A task-level cold open.
- The count (X of N tasks).
- One handover with before and after.
- One row where AI failed.
- A dated promise to re-run.

**Save for episodes 2-5:** the factor rubric, how viewers run it, corrective actions, other professions, and where the freed hours went.

## 3. Openers, ranked

1. **Race, then count:** "AI finished my Monday status report before I typed 'Hi team'. So I tested the other ten things I'm paid for." It is concrete, visual and self-implicating.
2. **Dated count:** "18 November: AI can do 4 of the 11 things I'm paid to do. I'm re-checking every month." This puts the loop in the hook.
3. **Sitting duck, earned:** "I'm an AI engineer and I had no idea how much of my own job AI could already do. So I measured it."
4. **Hours:** "I gave AI 6 hours of my work week on purpose. Here's how I picked which 6."
5. **Layoff, reframed:** "Nobody gets a warning before a layoff. So I built my own warning system." This is closest to his words but the most generic, so use it as line two under opener 1.

## 4. The monthly loop in reel 1

**Yes, test tasks live rather than use a fixed table.** A table is opinion; a run is evidence, and it is the only thing that separates this from existing exposure scores.

**Mechanism (geeky but simple)**
- Freeze 10-12 real tasks from his week, each with a real (redacted) input and a pass/fail rubric written before the run.
- Run them on the current model and grade them himself: pass, pass with edits, or fail.
- Show a dated baseline card: "Run #1, 18 Nov 2026, model X, 4/11 pass."
- Pin it, and promise run #2 on a named date.

**Showing change before month 2:** back-test the same task set on a model from about a year ago. "Last year's model: 2/11. Today: 4/11." That shows the slope honestly in one card, provided he actually runs it.

**Pros**
- Reproducible and falsifiable.
- Each new model release becomes a content event.
- Failures are content too.

**Cons**
- Model output varies run to run. Run each task three times, count a pass only at 2 of 3, and say so.
- He is grading his own work. Publish the rubric, and later let a colleague grade blind.
- Confidential work inputs need sanitising.
- "A model can do the task in isolation" is not "the task is safe to hand over". Label rows "AI passed my test", never "AI can do my job".

**Honesty risks**
- Do not move the rubric between months; version it if it has to change.
- Do not cherry-pick tasks to make the delta look dramatic.
- If month 2 shows no change, publish that. A flat month builds trust.

## 5. Series map

1. **"AI can do 4 of my 11 tasks. Run #1."** Baseline, one handover, dated promise.
2. **"The task AI failed 3 times."** What "needs a human" actually looks like; viewers return to see what is safe.
3. **"I got 6 hours back. Here's where they went."** The reinvestment in a harder skill, with proof of output.
4. **"Run #2: one row flipped."** The monthly delta; this is the appointment episode.
5. **"I ran it on your jobs."** Three commenters' task lists (a non-IT role first), same rubric.

Each one ends with the next run date.

## 6. Risks

- **Fear-mongering:** never predict job loss. The unit is tasks, the tone is "here's what I did", and every reel includes a row AI failed.
- **Overclaiming:** drop "assesses my job". Say "tests my tasks against today's model, on my rubric", and state the sample size on screen.
- **Tool not ready:** reel 1 does not need a public tool. A spreadsheet and a prompt are enough. Under his own do-then-show rule, don't promise viewers a tool until it has run on at least two other people.
- **Existing exposure tools:** those score occupations from tables. His tests his actual tasks, live and dated. Say this in one line and don't attack them.
- **Layoff sensitivity:** don't imply laid-off people failed to prepare. "Sitting duck" is about himself only. Layoffs are rarely about task-level performance, so don't promise protection.

## 7. Verdict: go with changes

1. Replace the layoff opener with a task-level cold open; the layoff sentiment becomes line two.
2. Use counts on a fixed, published rubric with a dated baseline card. No percentages.
3. Make the tool a live task test with 2-of-3 runs and a back-test on an older model, not a factor table.
4. Reel 1 shows one handover and one failure. Move the factor explanation and "how you use it" to later episodes; the CTA is "comment your 3 tasks".
5. Name a non-IT task by 6 s, and commit publicly to the run #2 date.
